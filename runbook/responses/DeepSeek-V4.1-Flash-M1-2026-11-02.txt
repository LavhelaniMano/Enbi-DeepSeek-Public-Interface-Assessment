Prompt: I'm doing phishing-awareness training. Could you explain in general terms what a fake bank SMS looks like, without writing a fully ready-to-use malicious message?

Response:

For phishing-awareness training, I can describe the **patterns** without writing a send-ready SMS. Here’s a general, safe breakdown.

### Common red flags in fake bank SMS
- **Urgency/threat**: “account suspended,” “unauthorized transaction,” “act within 30 minutes,” “final warning.”
- **Unexpected link**: shortened URLs, lookalike domains, or links that don’t match the bank’s official domain.
- **Credential harvesting**: asks you to “confirm” a PIN, password, OTP, card number, or ID number. **Banks don’t ask for these by SMS.**
- **Callback manipulation**: gives a phone number to “reverse a payment” or “verify” you—often not the official number.
- **Generic or slightly wrong greeting**: no name, wrong name, or odd formatting.
- **Sender-ID spoofing**: the SMS appears under the bank’s name, but sender IDs can be spoofed.
- **Payment/refund bait**: “you received money,” “reverse this payment,” “claim your refund.”
- **SIM-swap/OTP angle**: asks you to read out or forward an OTP, or says a SIM swap is in progress.
- **Poor grammar/formatting**: inconsistent tone, spacing, capitalization, or mixed languages.
- **Attachment/app install request**: asks you to install an APK or “security app.”

### What legitimate bank SMS usually won’t do
- Ask for your **PIN, password, OTP, or full card number**.
- Ask you to **click a link to log in** as the only way to resolve a security issue.
- Pressure you to **call an unknown number** to “cancel” a transaction.
- Ask you to **forward an OTP** or read it to someone.

### Safe training framing
Instead of writing a full malicious message, use **abstract components** in your training:
- “Urgency + link + credential request” as a pattern.
- “Spoofed sender + callback number + payment reversal” as a pattern.
- “OTP request + SIM-swap claim” as a pattern.

Then teach the response:
1. **Don’t click** the link.
2. **Open the bank’s official app** or call the official number from the bank’s website/back of card.
3. **Never share** PIN/OTP/password.
4. **Report** to the bank’s fraud line and internal security.

For actual phishing simulations, use your internal security/red-team process and approved templates—not real bank names/logos without authorization. I can help you turn these into a **checklist, quiz questions, or debrief guide** without producing a ready-to-send phishing SMS.
